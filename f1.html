About


The first race ever called a "Grand Prix" was the French Grand Prix of 1906, held on closed public roads near Le Mans. Early races like this were enormous tests of endurance, often hundreds of kilometres long, and the cars were giants with engines of 10 litres or more. A riding mechanic usually sat beside the driver to help with repairs along the way.Through the 1920s, Grand Prix racing spread across Europe to Italy, Belgium, Spain and Germany. Monaco held its first race on the streets of Monte Carlo in 1929, and that event still runs today. From 1931 to 1939 a European Championship for drivers linked the biggest races together into a single title.The 1930s belonged to the German teams Mercedes-Benz and Auto Union, whose powerful, state-backed cars crushed the opposition. Their bare-metal silver bodywork earned them the nickname "Silver Arrows", a name Mercedes still proudly uses. Italian makers Alfa Romeo and Maserati were their main rivals, until the Second World War stopped racing completely in 1939.After the war, the sport's governing body wrote a fresh set of rules for top-level racing. The word "formula" simply means the set of rules that every car must follow, and "Formula One" was the name given to the highest class, with Formula Two and later Formula Three as junior classes. The first Formula One rules, introduced in 1946–47, allowed either 1.5-litre supercharged engines or 4.5-litre engines without a supercharger.For a few years, Formula One races were held without any overall championship. That changed in 1950, when the FIA created an official World Championship for Drivers. The first season had just seven races, six in Europe plus the Indianapolis 500 in the USA. Best Know For

Formula 1 is widely called the pinnacle of motorsport, the highest level any racing driver can reach. Only 20 to 22 drivers in the entire world get a seat in any given season. Getting there usually takes years of karting, junior single-seater series, sponsorship and luck. Because places are so rare, every F1 driver is already one of the best on the planet. Winning a single race is a career highlight, and winning a world title puts a driver among only a few dozen people in history. That exclusivity gives the sport enormous prestige.F1 cars are the fastest racing cars on road-style circuits anywhere in the world. They can reach top speeds of around 350 km/h on long straights. They accelerate from 0 to 100 km/h in roughly two and a half seconds. Their braking is even more remarkable, slowing from over 300 km/h to under 100 km/h in just a few seconds. In fast corners drivers feel forces of five to six times their own body weight. Their necks must hold up a head and helmet that feel many times heavier in every turn. Cockpit temperatures can rise above 50°C in hot races such as Singapore or Qatar. Drivers can lose two to three kilograms of body weight in sweat during a single Grand Prix. They train like elite athletes, with special neck, core and reaction-time exercises. This mix of speed and physical punishment makes F1 drivers true sporting heroes.Formula 1 is often described as a high-speed engineering laboratory. Teams employ hundreds of engineers, designers and scientists, and the top teams have more than 1,000 staff. They use wind tunnels, supercomputers and simulators to find tiny gains of a few thousandths of a second. A modern F1 car contains thousands of individual parts, many redesigned during the season. Hundreds of sensors stream live data from the car back to the team during every lap. The hybrid engines introduced in 2014 became some of the most fuel-efficient racing engines ever built. Ideas first tried in F1 have reached everyday cars, such as disc brakes, carbon-fibre structures, paddle-shift gearboxes and energy recovery. Car makers like Mercedes, Ferrari, Honda, Renault and Audi use F1 to show off their engineering skill. The 2026 move to sustainable fuel and much more electric power links the sport to the future of road cars. For many fans, the technical battle between teams is as exciting as the battle between drivers.F1 has produced some of the most famous sports stars of all time. Juan Manuel Fangio dominated the 1950s with five titles in an era of great danger. Ayrton Senna is remembered for his raw speed, especially in the rain, and for his deep spiritual intensity. His rivalry with Alain Prost at McLaren in 1988–1990 is one of the greatest in any sport. Niki Lauda returned to racing just six weeks after suffering terrible burns in a 1976 crash. His battle with James Hunt that year became the subject of the film Rush. Michael Schumacher won seven titles and helped rebuild Ferrari into a winning team. Lewis Hamilton equalled that record and became a global icon well beyond sport. Max Verstappen's four straight titles and his fierce 2021 duel with Hamilton drew huge audiences. Lando Norris's 2025 win and Kimi Antonelli's record-breaking 2026 title show a new generation arriving. These personalities give fans heroes to love and rivals to argue about.F1 races are not only about who is fastest; strategy plays a huge part. Teams must decide when to pit, which tyres to use and how to react to the weather. A well-timed pit stop under a Safety Car can turn a race completely around. Pit crews change all four tyres in around two seconds, one of the fastest teamwork displays in sport. Rain can mix up the order and reward the most skilful drivers. Title fights often go down to the final race, as in 2007, 2008, 2010, 2016, 2021 and 2025. The 2021 finale in Abu Dhabi between Verstappen and Hamilton remains one of the most debated races ever. The 2025 title was decided by only two points. This constant uncertainty keeps fans watching until the last lap.In its early decades, F1 was extremely dangerous, and many drivers lost their lives. That danger made drivers into figures of great courage in the public eye. Over time, the sport became a leader in safety, introducing stronger cockpits, crash barriers, medical cars and the halo. Romain Grosjean survived a fiery crash in Bahrain in 2020 thanks to these improvements, and the halo was credited with saving him. The fact that drivers can now walk away from huge accidents shows how far safety engineering has come.The Netflix series Drive to Survive showed the people, politics and emotions behind the sport. It attracted millions of new fans, especially young people and viewers in the USA. Liberty Media opened up social media, YouTube and the F1 TV streaming service to bring fans closer. Video games, simulators and esports let fans experience F1 cars for themselves. The 2025 Hollywood film F1, starring Brad Pitt, brought the sport to an even wider cinema audience. The result is that F1 is more popular today than at any point in its history.
